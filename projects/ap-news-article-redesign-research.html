01 · Executive Summary

Five Headline Findings

Across 6 user interviews and 10 stakeholder interviews, five findings emerge with consistent, cross-source signal. These are not isolated complaints — they represent structural issues that affect reader experience, editorial quality, and business outcomes simultaneously.

01

The article page is trying to do too much

Ads, donation prompts, recirculation modules, newsletter sign-ups, and editorial content compete for the same space. Users and editors both describe this as the dominant problem. On a large desktop monitor, one stakeholder showed two sentences of article text visible above the fold.

02

Recirculation needs a strategy grounded in evidence

We have recirculation tools — related stories, hub peeks, inline links, third-party modules — but no unified strategy for how they work together, where they live, or what the evidence says about their effectiveness. Testing and data should drive placement and prioritization decisions, not convention.

03

AP's strongest asset — its journalism — is undersold

Users consistently cited AP's writing quality, fact-checking rigor, and reporter credibility as the reason they chose AP over competitors. None of these qualities are surfaced prominently in the article page design. Bylines, beat assignments, and in-text fact checks are buried or incidental.

04

Editorial tooling creates quality ceilings

CMS limitations — blind stacking, no scheduled publishing, broken preview, no duplicate image detection, and a broken pull quote component — mean editors cannot reliably produce the visual quality they intend. Better reader experience requires better author experience.

05

Loyal users deserve a meaningfully better experience

Our most engaged users — donors, daily readers, registered accounts — currently receive the same experience as first-time visitors. There is an opportunity to build a product that recognizes, rewards, and deepens loyalty through quality: less friction, better personalization, and a sense that AP knows who they are.

02 · Key Challenges

What Is Getting in the Way

These eight challenges were surfaced across both user and stakeholder interviews. They represent root causes, not symptoms — addressing surface-level design without resolving these will produce limited improvement.

⚡

Article element injection needs better governance

Ads, editorial modules, donations, and recirculation are injected by separate teams without a shared coordination framework. The result is articles where injected content outweighs editorial content on screen.

👁

Preview doesn't reflect production

Editors make layout decisions based on a preview that doesn't show real ads or injected modules. Layout problems are discovered only after publication.

📐

The featured template barely differs from standard

Centered content and a larger hero are the only differences. There are no full-bleed options, no text overlay, no configurable carousel or pull quote. Editors seeking more must go to the immersive team, which has limited capacity.

🔇

Push notifications are broadly rejected

The majority of interviewed users have disabled all push notifications — not just news. Email newsletters are the preferred communication channel for this cohort.

🎯

Donors are shown the same prompts as non-donors

Registered donors experience the same donation pop-ups as anonymous visitors. This is a recognition failure that actively frustrates the users most likely to advocate for AP.

🗺

Article optimization has no clear owner

Tasks like adding a video that publishes hours after a story, or ensuring related stories are accurate, fall in the gap between editorial, digital, and product — with no systematic process to catch them.

🔖

Save and bookmark are less visible and effective than they could be

Some users have built their own workarounds — emailing articles, keeping browser tabs open — because in-app save features aren't prominent enough to be trusted or discovered easily. The feature exists but its placement and promotion underserve demand.

📱

Mobile/desktop is a more useful lens than web/app

Platform decisions are framed as web vs. app, but mobile web and mobile app share a form factor. Most decisions should apply to both mobile contexts, not just one platform.

03 · User Types

Who We're Designing For

These user types emerged from behavioral patterns across both user and stakeholder interviews. They are not exhaustive personas — they are lenses for design decision-making. The most important strategic distinction cuts across all of them: anonymous/passive vs. registered/invested.

👤

The Anonymous Casual Reader

Arrives via shared link, search, or social. Reads one story and leaves. No account, no brand investment — may not even know they're on AP News. Represents the largest share of article page traffic and the biggest recirculation opportunity.

🔁

The Habitual Loyal Reader

Checks AP multiple times daily, has the app, thinks of AP as their primary national news source. High brand affinity and strong product expectations. Notices when content feels stale or layout feels cluttered. Likely to advocate for AP — or quietly churn if the experience degrades.

✉️

The Newsletter-First Reader

Engages primarily through email digests. Clicks into stories from newsletters but rarely opens the app independently. Deliberate about limiting digital noise — has often disabled push notifications entirely. The article page is their landing experience, not a starting point.

📚

The Multi-Source Power Reader

Reads AP alongside FT, WSJ, Politico, and others as part of a curated personal news diet. Values AP's neutrality and writing quality. Highly critical of visual clutter and design trends they perceive as dumbing down the experience. Often saves articles for later reading.

🔬

The Niche Interest Reader

Comes to AP primarily for a specific vertical — religion, science and medicine, entertainment, or niche sports. The general top-news feed doesn't serve them well. Personalization features and topic-specific newsletters are their most valued touchpoints.

🤝

The Monthly Donor

Financially committed to AP, motivated by belief in independent journalism and concerns about press freedom. Tends to be highly engaged and vocal. Currently receives the same experience as an anonymous visitor — including donation prompts — which actively undermines their sense of recognition and loyalty.

📱

The App Power User

Actively customizes navigation, follows topics, and has strong preferences about their reading experience. A smaller but highly loyal cohort. Stakeholders identify this group as distinct from passive web visitors — they require a different design posture: less recirculation pressure, more depth and personalization.

🖥

The Wire Customer / B2B Reader

Not a consumer user, but shapes how editorial and CMS decisions get made. Editors think about the wire feed as much as the consumer product, which creates tension in how stories are structured and presented. Understanding this distinction matters when evaluating why certain editorial defaults exist.

Session Overview

The session was structured in eight parts: welcome and agenda, silent research review, tool demo, desktop article build, break, mobile article build, prioritization of actions, and wrap-up. Participants used stamps (thumbs up/down) to react to pre-populated research findings, stickies to add commentary and questions, and a component library to build their own vision of an ideal article page for both desktop and mobile. All votes were counted after the session and directly inform design prioritization.

04 · What Participants Built

Participants each chose a user type and built their ideal desktop article page using a component library, then adapted it for mobile. The builds were discussed as a group and key patterns extracted.

W4-B1
Design Pattern
Every participant placed a donation module at or near the end of the article — several explicitly framing it as "appreciation for the journalism" rather than a top-of-page interruption.
"The donation prompt can be last on the article! It's often thought of as appreciation for the journalism, so it makes sense for readers to finish first." — Dessi
Design Implication

There is consensus across the group — and alignment with user interview data — that donation prompts belong after the article, not before or during it. Moving donation modules to post-read position is worth A/B testing against current placement, with engagement rate and conversion tracked separately.

W4-B2
Design Pattern
Participants consistently placed "More from AP News" recirculation above the donation module but below the article body — with multiple discrete sections rather than a single large block.
"Multiple recirc blocks but with the following criteria: fewer articles — three really good and relevant articles is better than six uninteresting or unrelated ones. Discrete sections: one on related topics to the article, one on most-commented or most-read content." — workshop sticky
Design Implication

Quality over quantity in recirculation is a consistent theme from both the builds and the broader discussion. The design direction: 2–3 highly relevant related articles in a clearly labeled section, separate from a "most read" or "trending" section, rather than a single undifferentiated module. Relevance matching was flagged as a prerequisite — poor taxonomy undermines the whole mechanic.

W4-B3
Design Pattern
Newsletter sign-up modules appeared in most builds — but positioned mid-article or at article end, never at the top. One participant proposed making the sign-up a gated feature for the audio "Listen to this article" experience.
"Create a free account to listen to this article." — component placed in multiple builds. "Listening to the article tends to be one of the first prompts at other publications — it should encourage registration (we shouldn't let readers listen without any requirements!)" — rsturm
Design Implication

Audio as a registration gate is a novel and potentially high-value idea. If AP's audio content is genuinely human-narrated (not AI), offering it as a registered-user feature gives the registration prompt a clear, immediate value exchange: "create a free account to listen." This reframes audio from a buried embed into a registration mechanic.

W4-B4
Design Pattern
Participants consistently simplified the article action bar — proposing icon-only share, save, and comment actions rather than labeled buttons, reducing visual clutter near the byline.
"Changed the buttons — would love to introduce 'Saving Articles' as a registered user feature underneath the byline. Could we get away with just the icons for share, save, and comment rather than the words themselves to save space?" — rsturm
Design Implication

The action bar (Donate | Share | Save | Comment) is currently text-heavy. An icon-only approach — with labels appearing on hover or tap — would reduce visual noise while preserving discoverability. "Saving Articles" framed as a registered user benefit directly under the byline is a tight, low-friction value proposition for registration.

W4-B5
Design Pattern
White space was consistently prioritized in builds — with one participant hypothesizing directly that maximizing white space would lead to more articles per session by improving the reading experience.
"I think maximizing white space is critical. If we visit NYT, Atlantic, other publications with strong user experience and design aesthetics — white space is used abundantly. Hypothesis is that a visit to an improved article experience will lead to more articles per session than including everything possible in one page." — workshop sticky
Design Implication

This is worth testing directly. The hypothesis — that visual restraint correlates with deeper engagement — is consistent with what high-quality editorial publishers have demonstrated. Pull quotes were also specifically noted as a whitespace tool that can improve article completion when used well. Designs that test generous whitespace vs. current density against scroll depth and pages-per-session metrics would validate or challenge this hypothesis.

05 · Key Tensions the Workshop Surfaced

Several productive disagreements emerged during the session. These are not problems to resolve in the workshop — they are decisions to make with data.

↔ Tension

Ad density vs. reading experience

Revenue participants want to preserve ad load — particularly for anonymous users who generate most ad revenue. Design participants want to reduce visual clutter. The resolution likely lies in cleaner ad presentation (full-width, color-backed) rather than fewer ads.

↔ Tension

Mid-article recirculation: test or skip?

One participant argued strongly against mid-article recirculation on mobile, preferring end-of-article placement. Others referenced CNN data supporting persistent recirculation. This is an A/B test, not a debate to resolve in a workshop.

↔ Tension

Registered user experience: visible or invisible?

Should loyal users see a meaningfully different-looking page, or just a cleaner version of the same page (fewer prompts, no donor asks)? The group leaned toward invisible recognition — suppression rather than differentiation.

↔ Tension

Ad-lite as donor perk: viable or too costly?

Eric Vora proposed gating ad-lite behind a higher monthly donation threshold. The revenue team noted this would require the threshold to offset the lost ad revenue. No consensus reached — needs financial modeling.

02 · What Participants Built — Patterns Across Builds

Across six individual builds (Dave Clark, Brian, Sophia, Gennise, Milly/Audrius, Paige/Laura), several strong patterns emerged in how participants designed their ideal article page.

W3-B1
Design Pattern
Every build deprioritized donation prompts to the end of the article — with multiple participants proposing a contextual AP mission statement as a bridge before the donation ask, rather than leading with the dollar amount.
"I was thinking the least invasive donation ask possible — mostly just a Donate button. Don't think we should have amounts, but should definitely have a Donate button." — seppolito. "Insert a box here that talks about AP brand: Independent news with no paywall. AP has no corporate shareholders, etc. (this would set up the ask below)." — Laura McNeill. "Something building awareness about AP's mission before the donate box." — Laura McNeill
Design Implication

A two-part end-of-article treatment — (1) AP mission/brand statement, (2) donation CTA — emerged as the consensus model across builds. The brand statement primes the emotional reason to give before presenting the ask. Some participants wanted minimal donation UI (button only, no pre-selected amounts); others wanted a full module. This should be A/B tested.

W3-B2
Design Pattern
Multiple builds included a reporter/byline box near the article end — a brief profile of the journalist who wrote the story, connecting the reader to the human behind the reporting before the donation ask.
"A box here that describes the reporter and the AP / [work they did to get this story] CTA." — Laura McNeill. "A box here that describes the reporter and the [work they did]." — Laura McNeill (in both desktop and mobile builds)
Design Implication

A reporter card at article end — brief bio, beat, photo — humanizes AP's journalism and creates a natural bridge to the "support this kind of journalism" donation ask. This pattern exists at ProPublica, The Guardian, and The Atlantic. It's a relatively contained design component that could be introduced as part of the featured template initially.

W3-B3
Design Pattern
Dave Clark's build was explicitly designed for the anonymous casual user — emphasizing story flow, deprioritizing embeds, and creating a pathway toward newsletter sign-up and commenting rather than donation, based on where this user is in the funnel.
"I built this with more of a casual user in mind. Someone who has come to the page and didn't include any embedded photos/videos. Wanted to give users a non-cluttered experience... explore other AP content and move down the funnel: commenting, registration." — Dave Clark. "Similar to my desktop design, emphasis on story flow, prioritized embeds for moving users down the funnel starting with the least aggressive: newsletter signups." — Dave Clark (mobile)
Design Implication

Dave Clark's funnel-aware design approach — matching the article's CTA to the user's likely stage (anonymous → newsletter → registration → comment → donate) — is the most strategically coherent build in the session. It avoids asking for too much too soon and builds the relationship progressively. This maps well to the journey-based personalization concept from the Workshop 1 session.

W3-B4
Design Pattern
Sophia built for a first-time visitor with no account — focusing on promoting what registration unlocks, keeping recirculation and commenting visible, and deliberately avoiding donation prompts for this cohort.
"For someone who is clicking on an AP story for the first time and does not yet have an account. My focus was to promote the things we can offer them if they sign up (registration and newsletter) and hopefully encourage a second click. Plus keeping commenting as a constant at the bottom of every page. Ideally this page wouldn't have any donate pop-ups." — Sophia
Design Implication

Sophia's build makes the strongest case for suppressing the donation ask on first-visit article pages entirely. For users with no account and no prior AP relationship, a donation ask is premature — registration and newsletter conversion are higher-value actions at this stage. Confirmed by the conversion data: 55% of registrations happen on the article page, but first-time visitors may need to read a second article before they're ready to give.

W3-B5
Design Pattern
Paige/Laura's build focused on the loyal reader — designing a minimal, clean experience where the reader gets through the article smoothly, then encounters a well-framed donation ask. No pop-ups. "If you made it to the bottom and enjoyed the ride, simple donate at the end."
"I tried to not put too many CTAs so similar to desktop I don't want to annoy those who aren't motivated to give — they will read more anyway because they are already very loyal. At the bottom is a donate module, but I was less worried about recirc because curious if those folks are already in the app so prob they left it at the very end." — Paige/Laura
Design Implication

A minimal experience for loyal users — fewer CTAs, no mid-article interruptions, a single clean donation ask at the end — is the consistent recommendation from multiple builds. This cohort is already converted; the goal is not to convert them but to not irritate them. The recirculation concern is valid: app users may not need aggressive in-article recirculation because they're already in the product ecosystem.

W3-B6
Design Pattern
Multiple participants proposed making "Sign Up" or "Create an Account" significantly more prominent — with one suggesting a square or bold CTA treatment — noting that the current registration prompt feels like a small hyperlink buried below more prominent elements.
"Can we make 'Sign up' or 'Make an account' way [bigger]? Instead of a reg module, [make it] a larger CTA." — Anonymous participant. "Listen to the article could be a smaller module up top so the user knows." — board annotation. "Explore personalized data options, value beyond simply reading and saving." — Dario Lopez (for logged-in users)
Design Implication

Registration CTA size and prominence is under-designed relative to its strategic importance. A bold, clearly labeled "Create free account" button — not a link, not a modal — tested against the current approach could meaningfully impact registration rate. For logged-in users, Dario's suggestion of surfacing "what you can do with your account" (personalization, saving, newsletter) as a post-read module reinforces loyalty.

03 · Key Tensions from This Session

↔ Tension

Donation ask: minimal or contextual?

seppolito argued for the least invasive approach — a button only, no pre-selected amounts. Laura McNeill argued for a contextual module with AP's mission statement as context. Both agree it belongs at the end; the question is how much friction is appropriate before the ask.

Recirculation for loyal users: necessary or noise?

Paige/Laura argued loyal app users don't need aggressive recirculation because they're already in the ecosystem. Dave Clark built recirculation in prominently. The right answer likely varies by platform (app vs. web) and should be tested independently.

Sophia's build suppressed donation prompts entirely for first-time visitors. Most other builds included them. The data on conversion by visit depth would resolve this — but we don't currently have it.

↔ Tension

Ad strategy: audit first, then redesign?

Josh Cornfield proposed a competitive ad audit before locking in ad placement decisions. Others moved forward with ad placements in builds. The audit would provide evidence; skipping it risks optimizing for a metric (CPM) while sacrificing a larger one (total user value).

Consensus on User Profiles

Across all builds in both workshops, participants naturally designed different experiences for three distinct user types — without being asked to. The pattern was consistent enough to constitute a design direction.

Anonymous · First visit

Newsletter + commenting first

No donation prompt. Clean reading experience. Register to comment is the primary conversion ask. Move them toward a second visit before asking for financial support.

Registered · Return visitor

Lighter treatment, donation introduced

Fewer interruptions. Donation appears once, at the end, framed around AP's mission. The relationship is established — now earn the financial ask.

Monthly Donor

Recognition, not asks

No donation prompts. No registration prompts. Show them newsletters, topics, and niche coverage — not what they already give. Their loyalty is demonstrated; treat it that way.

The minimum viable version requires no new data: use existing account state — donor, registered, or anonymous — to suppress the wrong CTA for the wrong person. Journey-based adaptation (responding to visit depth and behavior) is the longer-term opportunity.

Three Tensions Design Alone Can't Resolve

These are decisions that need data, financial modeling, or cross-functional alignment — not design direction.

↔ Tension 01

Ads are essential — but how they're presented needs to evolve

Ad revenue is the primary financial engine of the article page, and our Revenue team is deeply invested in protecting it — rightly so. The tension isn't whether to have ads; it's whether the current mix of placements, formats, and densities is actually optimized for total revenue or just for CPM in isolation. Both workshops raised the question of whether better-harmonized, more intentionally placed ads — ones that don't mimic editorial content, auto-play video, or compete with the reading experience — could drive higher engagement and therefore higher downstream revenue through more page views, registrations, and donations. This is a shared problem for Revenue and Product to work through together, with data, not a design decision to make unilaterally.

↔ Tension 02

Should first-time visitors see a donation prompt at all?

Sophia's build removed donation prompts entirely for first-time visitors. Most other builds included them. The data on conversion rate by visit depth would settle this — but we don't currently have it. Asking for a donation on someone's first visit may be the right thing to do at scale, or it may be suppressing the relationship before it starts. This is a revenue and product strategy decision, not a design one.

↔ Tension 03

Is the ad-lite donor perk financially viable?

Eric Vora (Workshop 2) proposed that donors above a monthly giving threshold should receive an ad-free or ad-reduced experience — with the threshold set to offset lost ad revenue. Multiple users in interviews expressed willingness to increase their donation specifically to reduce ads. This requires financial modeling: what is the average ad revenue generated by a monthly donor? What threshold makes the exchange revenue-neutral or positive? Design can build the experience; Revenue needs to determine if the math works.

Cross-Reference: Confirmations & Tensions

Where user and stakeholder findings align, confidence is high. Where they diverge, there is a design decision to make. Four of each are worth naming explicitly.

✓ Confirmed across both sources

Audio embeds are ignored

Users assume they are AI-generated. Kim independently surfaced the same insight from the editorial side. Strong signal for audio redesign — change the CTA, move it to the top, and make human authorship explicit.

↔ Tension to resolve

Recirculation vs. reading experience

Stakeholders see recirculation as the primary article goal. Users (esp. Anna) actively try not to be distracted. Resolution is position and presentation — recirculation that doesn't feel like an interruption, placed where users naturally pause.

✓ Confirmed across both sources

Ads are the primary complaint — but tolerated

Both user interviews and stakeholder feedback forms cite ad volume and presentation. Users accept ads as the cost of free content but draw the line at ads that mimic editorial content and video ads that auto-play.

↔ Tension to resolve

Personalization is desired and feared simultaneously

Users want topic surfacing but multiple users independently expressed concern about the filter bubble. Stakeholders have not surfaced this concern internally. Design must make the editorial-first model explicit in how personalization is framed and communicated.

✓ Confirmed across both sources

Related stories are sought early, not at the end

At least one user's first action was to look for related stories before reading the lead article. Stakeholder CNN data shows 20–30% engagement when related content follows the reader persistently. Consensus: move it up.

↔ Tension to resolve

Featured treatment as prestige vs. storytelling tool

Editors sometimes request featured treatment as recognition of effort ("feather in the cap") rather than because the visual assets warrant it. Dave Clark named this explicitly. Design guidance — and documented templates — can help reframe the decision around visual suitability.

✓ Confirmed across both sources

Save/bookmark demand is real, discoverability is the problem

Users have built workarounds. Stakeholders confirm the feature exists but hasn't been meaningfully promoted. This is a visibility and trust problem, not a feature gap — but it needs to be treated with the urgency of a missing feature.

↔ New from stakeholders (not surfaced by users)

CMS workflow problems are invisible to users but determine quality

Blind stacking, no scheduled publishing, broken preview, and uncoordinated injection are upstream causes of many user-facing problems. Users experience the symptoms (visual clutter, irrelevant related stories) but can't name the cause. Fixing these doesn't show up in user satisfaction immediately — but sets the ceiling for what's possible.
